## 1. Why it exists

Two opposite risks sit side by side in a clinic:

- **Deleting too soon.** Medical, accounting and payroll records must be kept for periods set by law. Deleting them early is a breach of those laws, and a patient's request for erasure does not override them (Art. 17(3)(b) GDPR).
- **Keeping too long.** Everything without a legal reason to stay must go. Data kept "just in case" is a standing risk: it can leak, be misused or be asked for in a request.

The retention schedule resolves this category by category. It is also what lets the clinic keep a promise made in the patient notice: *exact periods are available on request*. The notice (module 2) gives the criteria, and this schedule gives the periods.

### How to set a period

For each category, ask in this order:

1. **Is there a legal minimum?** If yes, that is the period, and it is a floor. Name the law.
2. **Is there a legal maximum?** If yes, the period cannot exceed it (for example, the legal limit on keeping camera footage).
3. **Neither?** Then the period is set by real need, and it is a ceiling. Write the reason, and choose the shortest period the reason supports.
4. **Is the source verified?** If not, say so in the schedule and keep the period provisional.

### Four statuses, so nobody over-trusts a number

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Legal** | A legal rule that has been checked, with its source and the date checked |
| **Legal limit** | A legal maximum, with a shorter recommended period inside it |
| **Recommended** | No legal period; the clinic's own period, with the criterion stated |
| **To confirm** | A proposed period whose legal source has not been verified |

This is the single most useful habit in the document. A schedule that says "10 years" with no source looks authoritative and may be wrong. One that says "10 years, to confirm" tells the reader what still needs work.

### General rules

1. A legal period is a minimum. While a law requires data to be kept, it is not deleted, even if the person asks (Art. 17(3)(b) GDPR). Once the period ends, the data is deleted.
2. With no legal period, the period is a maximum. For categories without a legal period, the period in the table is the longest the data may be kept, set by how long it is really useful.
3. When the clock starts. The period runs from the event in the "Starts from" column, not from when the document was created, unless stated otherwise.
4. Annual check. Every January, the responsible person identifies data whose period has expired and deletes it. In practice, data can therefore stay at most 12 months past its period. Categories with short periods (CCTV, appointments) are deleted automatically.
5. Legal hold. Data is not deleted if there is a dispute, complaint, inspection, request from an authority or a data subject request being handled. The hold is noted and lifted when the matter ends.
6. Copies and duplicates. Avoid copies (phone photos, messaging apps, copies of ID documents). Copies that are not needed are deleted at once; copies that exist follow the period of the original document.
7. Backups. Data deleted from applications disappears from backups at the next rotation. Backups are kept for no more than 3 months.
8. Secure destruction. Paper: shredding, or destruction by an authorised firm, with a record. Electronic: permanent deletion, including from backups and devices. Every destruction is entered in the destruction register (section 4).
9. Providers. Providers that keep data for the clinic (the practice software, the accountant, the messaging provider) delete or return the data when the contract ends and confirm in writing.
10. Patient deletion requests. If the legal period has not ended, the request is refused with reasons and, where appropriate, processing is restricted. If it has ended, or there is no legal duty to keep the data, it is deleted (see the data subject rights procedure).

## 3. What was verified, and what was not

The schedule is honest about its own limits. As of **7 November 2026**:

**Checked against sources**

- **Accounting and payroll records (RET-05, RET-11(b)): 5 years.** Law no. 36/2023 amended Art. 25 of the Accounting Law no. 82/1991 (see a [published summary](https://www.fiscalitatea.ro/documente-care-vor-fi-pastrate-in-arhiva-timp-de-5-ani-de-contribuabili-conform-legii-362023-22429/), which is secondary; the current official text is still to be confirmed, OI-26). Registers, supporting documents and payroll statements are now kept for **5 years from 1 July of the year after the financial year** (previously 10 years for most records and 50 for payroll). The Ministry of Public Finance order that implements it (no. 1447/2023, published by the tax authority) gives one exception: supporting documents for assets with a useful life longer than 5 years are kept for that useful life. *The consolidated current text is still to be confirmed with the accountant.*
- **Camera footage for employees (RET-06).** Art. 5 of Law no. 190/2018 limits storage to 30 days, with exceptions. The clinic keeps footage for 7 days.

**Not verified**

- **Medical records (RET-01 to RET-04).** No single act giving the periods for a small outpatient practice could be found. Guidance published on the Romanian College of Physicians' GDPR site points to the National Archives Law no. 16/1996 and tells practices to list the retention period for each document category in their own register, without giving the periods. The 10-year and 5-year figures here are **recommendations marked "to confirm"**, to be checked with the public health authority, the professional college or a lawyer before the schedule is approved.
- **ANSPDCP Decision no. 52/2012 (video surveillance).** Only the draft text was found, with a 30-day maximum. The final text is still to be confirmed.

> **A correction worth showing.** An earlier draft of this schedule used the older 10-year accounting period, because the 2022 proposal to shorten it had not been confirmed as law. Checking the sources while writing this module showed it had been enacted in 2023. The schedule, the Romanian original and this page were corrected. This is exactly why every period carries a status and a "last checked" date.

## 4. Design decisions

**1. Status on every row.** Legal rules, legal limits, recommendations and unverified periods look different from each other in the table. Mixing them under one heading is the most common failure of retention schedules.

**2. Legal periods are floors; everything else is a ceiling.** Two rules, stated up front, handle most deletion requests: if the law says keep, refuse; if it does not, delete once the period ends.

**3. One clean-up a year, plus automation for the short ones.** CCTV and appointments are deleted automatically, which keeps them short without effort. Everything else is reviewed in a single annual run, so data stays at most 12 months beyond its period. A monthly spot check proves the automation still works.

**4. Legal hold.** Nothing is deleted while a dispute, complaint, inspection or data subject request is open. Without this rule, an annual clean-up can destroy evidence.

**5. Backups and copies are part of the schedule.** Deleting from the application does nothing if copies live on in backups, phone photos or messaging apps. The rules name them, and give backups a rotation (3 months at Clinic X).

**6. A 24-month cap on marketing contacts.** Consent can last for ever in principle, but the clinic should not keep a contact for ever. The GDPR sets no figure, so 24 months is a stated recommendation: after that the contact is deleted or asked to reconfirm. Proof of consent is kept for 3 years, the general limitation period, in case of a claim.

**7. A destruction register.** A deletion that is not recorded cannot be shown to a regulator. The register is short (date, what, how, who) and is part of the annual run.

**8. Consistency with the notice.** The notice describes retention by criteria. This schedule is the document the clinic hands over when a patient asks for the exact period.

## 5. Romanian national layer

> **Romania-specific points** (the structure of the schedule applies anywhere in the EU)
>
> - **Accounting and payroll.** Art. 25 of Law no. 82/1991, as amended by Law no. 36/2023: 5 years from 1 July of the following year. See section 3 above. *Last checked 7 November 2026; confirm with the accountant.*
> - **Medical documentation.** The National Archives Law no. 16/1996 is the archiving framework; the specific periods for outpatient records are **to be confirmed**.
> - **Employee CCTV.** Art. 5 of Law no. 190/2018: no more than 30 days, with exceptions in law.
> - **Video surveillance generally.** ANSPDCP Decision no. 52/2012. *To be confirmed: final text.*
> - **Limitation period.** 3 years under Art. 2517 of the Civil Code, used for proof of consent and for data subject requests.
