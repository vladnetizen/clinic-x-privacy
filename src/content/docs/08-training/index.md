## 1. Why it exists

Most breaches in a small clinic are human: a wrong recipient, a password shared to save time, a request that nobody passed on. Module 6 showed one. Rules on paper do not prevent them. Staff who know what to do in the first minute do.

Training also answers a practical question from an inspector, an insurer or a partner: *How do you know your staff were told?* The answer is a plan and a signed record, not a certificate from a course.

Three principles keep it small enough to survive:

- **Short and real.** 45 minutes, using the practice's own documents and situations. No platform, no slides for their own sake.
- **Role first.** A receptionist and a nurse meet different risks. The role part is 15 to 20 minutes on exactly those.
- **No grading.** The mini-test finds what to explain better. The record names the questions that needed clarifying, not a score per person.

## 2. Who is trained, when and on what

| Group | When | Topics | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| New staff (employees, collaborators, trainees) | Before they get access to data. The confidentiality undertaking is signed at the session. | Patient notice; the 10 rules; professional secrecy; accounts and passwords; what an incident is and whom to tell; what a patient request looks like. | 45 minutes, plus the role part |
| All staff | Once a year (December, with the incident drill) and after any incident or major change. | The 10 rules refreshed; real incident stories; changes to procedures; the 5-question mini-test. | 45 minutes |
| Role part: clinical staff (physician, nurse) | On joining and every year. | Secrecy and Art. 9 GDPR; records of patients in care only; talking to relatives; handing over results; approved ways to take clinical images. | 15 to 20 minutes extra |
| Role part: reception | On joining and every year. | Checking identity; discretion at the desk; appointment texts without medical detail; recognising a request and passing it on within one working day; cameras and the notice. | 15 to 20 minutes extra |
| Role part: visiting physician | On starting and every year. | His position as an independent controller; his own device and account; own patients only; who tells whom after an incident; requests about his records. | 20 minutes extra |
| Security lead | When appointed and every year. | Incident and rights procedures; the controls register; ROPA and retention; provider reviews; legal updates. | 30 minutes, with the external adviser |

The session is led by Dr. A, with the external privacy adviser for the first one. The programme covers everyone with access to data, including the visiting physician, who is an independent controller for his own patients but works in the practice's rooms and systems.

## 3. The 45-minute session

| Minutes | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Why this matters | Two minutes on what health data is worth to others, and three on the practice's own near miss (a misdirected e-mail, caught in time). |
| 5–15 | The 10 rules | Go through the rules on the Staff Pack. For each rule, one real situation from the practice. Staff say which rule applies. |
| 15–25 | Spotting and reporting an incident | Walk through the near miss (a misdirected e-mail) as the procedure would handle it: who is told, in what order, in what time. Practise the phone call to the incident lead. |
| 25–35 | A patient asks… | Three short requests (a copy of the file, “delete my data”, “stop the messages”). Staff say what they do in the first minute and who they tell. |
| 35–45 | Mini-test and questions | Five questions, orally or on paper. The aim is to find what needs clarifying, not to grade people. Sign the attendance record. |

Dr. A runs it on **Tuesday 19 January 2027**, together with the role parts and the signing of the confidentiality undertakings. The same outline is used for the annual session each December and for new joiners.

### Role parts

| Role part | Content |
|---|---|
| Reception | Recognising a request in everyday words (“I want a copy of my file”, “no more messages”); passing it on within one working day. Identity at the desk and on the phone. Discretion: screens, voices, calling patients by first name. Results by e-mail: password-protected file, recipient read back. Clean desk. Accompanying visitors. Cameras: what the notice says and what to answer. |
| Clinical staff | Open only the records of patients in care. Secrecy and what may be said to relatives. Handing results over in person or by the secure channel. Clinical images only by approved means. Rectification: supplementary statements and dated notes, not deletion. Locking the screen at each step away. |
| Visiting physician | His own responsibilities as an independent controller: his notice, his incidents, his requests. Encrypted device and own account. Access to his own patients only. Who calls whom after an incident involving shared data. Requests about his records: pass them to the request handler the same day. |

### Tips for the trainer

- Use real documents from the practice: the Staff Pack, the incident contact list and the request register.
- Ask staff to say what they would do before showing the answer.
- Close each session with one thing each person will do differently, written on the record.

## 4. The mini-test

Five questions at the end of each session. In the first year the practice uses Q1 to Q5, in the second Q6 to Q10, and then the bank starts over, with a new question added after every incident.

**Year 1, as handed out**

1. A caller asks for their test results over the phone. What do you do?
2. You have e-mailed one patient's results to another patient by mistake. What do you do?
3. A patient says: “Delete my data.” What do you do?
4. A colleague asks for your password “to finish quickly”. What do you do?
5. A relative asks for an adult patient's file. What do you do?

The full bank, with expected answers and where each answer is covered, is below.

| No. | Question | Expected answer | Covered in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | A caller asks for their test results over the phone. What do you do? | No health data goes to someone whose identity has not been checked. Invite the person to the practice or use the agreed secure channel. | Security policy 8; rights procedure 5 |
| Q2 | You have e-mailed one patient's results to another patient by mistake. What do you do? | Tell the incident lead at once, within 1 hour. Do not delete the e-mail and do not try to fix it alone. The lead decides the next steps (deletion request to the recipient, assessment, possible notification). | Incident procedure |
| Q3 | A patient says: “Delete my data.” What do you do? | It is a request to exercise a right. Write down who, what and when, and pass it to the request handler within one working day. Do not answer on the substance. | Rights procedure |
| Q4 | A colleague asks for your password “to finish quickly”. What do you do? | Do not give it. Accounts are individual. The colleague asks the security lead for access. If they insist, tell the security lead. | Security policy 4 |
| Q5 | A relative asks for an adult patient's file. What do you do? | Give nothing without the patient's written consent or a legal basis. Send the person to the physician in charge. | Security policy 8; professional secrecy |
| Q6 | An e-mail with an invoice attachment arrives from a sender you do not know. What do you do? | Do not open it or click any link. Tell the security lead. If you already opened it, say so at once, and do not switch the computer off or reconnect it until you are told. | Security policy 5; incident procedure |
| Q7 | You find a lab printout in the printer tray, and the reception screen shows a patient's record. What do you do? | Take the printout to the right file or the locked cabinet and lock the screen. Keep the desk clear of papers at the end of the day. If anything went missing, report it. | Security policy 7 |
| Q8 | An engineer wants to work alone in the archive room. What do you do? | Visitors and providers are accompanied in areas with personal data. Stay with them, or ask the security lead. | Security policy 7 |
| Q9 | A patient asks to see the camera footage of the waiting room. What do you do? | Footage is not shown at reception. It is a request for access: write it down and pass it to the request handler within one working day. | Rights procedure; CCTV notice |
| Q10 | Your phone, which has appointment texts, is lost. What do you do? | Tell the security lead at once, within 1 hour. Lock or wipe the phone if you can. The lead assesses what was on it. | Incident procedure; security policy 5 |

### How to tell it is working

| What to look at | Why it tells you something |
|---|---|
| Everyone trained before getting access | The basic promise of the programme. Any exception is written down and fixed. |

## 6. What was verified, and what was not

**Checked**

- **Art. 32(4) GDPR.** The controller must take steps so that anyone acting under its authority and having access to personal data processes it only on the controller's instructions, unless the law requires otherwise. Training and written rules are the usual evidence.

**Not verified, to be confirmed**

- **Romanian CNP safeguards.** Art. 4 of Law no. 190/2018 is understood to list regular training of staff among the safeguards for processing the national ID number. The exact current wording is to be confirmed.
- **Professional secrecy training.** Any duty to train medical staff on secrecy under health or professional-body rules was not checked.
- **How long to keep records.** There is no legal period. The clinic keeps them with the personnel file, which is an unverified recommendation in the retention schedule.

## 7. Design decisions

**1. Training that fits in a lunch break.** Forty-five minutes, once a year. A programme a three-person clinic can keep up beats an ambitious one it drops after six months.

**2. The first session is also a control.** It closes four items in the security register on the same day: the undertakings (SEC-29), training (SEC-30), the photography rule (SEC-25) and the briefing on the incident procedure (SEC-33).

**3. Role parts, not role courses.** The extra 15 to 20 minutes is where the real risks sit: results at the desk for reception, relatives at the bedside for the nurse.

**4. A question bank that rotates.** The same five questions every year teach nothing after year one. Ten questions, five per year, with a new one after each incident, keep the test tied to what actually happened.

**5. No scores per person.** A score invites hiding and gaming. The practice records what needed explaining, then explains it.

**6. Refresher after incidents, within two weeks.** A 10-minute session on what went wrong is the best training the practice will get. It is also recorded.

**7. The visiting physician is in.** He has his own patients, device and account. Leaving him out would leave the largest gap in the room.

**8. The record is the proof.** An inspector will ask what was done and when. Dates, topics, attendees and signatures answer that in a minute.

## 8. Romanian national layer

> **Romania-specific points** (the plan applies anywhere in the EU)
>
> - **National ID number.** Art. 4 of Law no. 190/2018 sets safeguards for processing the CNP, which include appropriate measures and, as understood, regular staff training. *To be confirmed: current text.*
> - **Employee monitoring.** Art. 5 of Law no. 190/2018 requires prior information of staff (and consultation of their representatives) before monitoring, including by video. The training does not replace that notice.
> - **Professional secrecy** of medical staff arises from the law and the rules of the profession. The undertaking supplements it. *To be confirmed: sources.*

## 9. Blank templates

The blank plan follows the structure above. Yellow fields are for you to complete.

**How to use it**

1. Name a training lead and an approver.
2. List who needs training, including collaborators and visiting professionals.
3. Adapt the 45-minute outline to your own documents and one real situation per rule.
4. Pick five questions from the bank, and add one of your own.
5. Run the first session within a month of adopting your policies, and have everyone sign the undertaking.
6. Keep the record with the personnel file, and repeat every year and after any incident.
