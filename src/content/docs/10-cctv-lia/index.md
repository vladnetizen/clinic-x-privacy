## 1. Why it exists

Legitimate interest is the only basis that fits cameras in a clinic: nobody can be asked for consent to walk through a door, and staff cannot give it freely. But the basis has to be earned. The controller must show three things in writing: the interest is real, the cameras are needed for it, and the people filmed are not affected more than is justified. That is the LIA.

In a clinic the balance is harder than in a shop. The waiting room tells a camera something about the people in it: they are at a medical practice. Some are ill, some are children. And the staff are in view for the whole working day, in an employment relationship where consent means little. So the module does three things beyond the usual template:

- **It limits the system before it justifies it.** Four cameras, common areas only, no audio, no live viewing, 7 days.
- **It is honest about the evidence.** Clinic X has had no serious incident. The case rests on the layout of the premises. The assessment says so, and says what would change the answer.
- **It follows the money to the people.** Staff, patients and children are each assessed, with what is done to reduce the effect on each.

## 3. The three-step test

### Step 1: is the interest legitimate?

| Purpose | Concrete reason | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Safety of patients and staff | Patients can be ill, anxious or confused; reception is staffed by one person who often leaves the desk; aggression or a fall in the waiting room could go unseen. | No serious incident so far. The reason is the layout and the risk, not a past event. |
| Protection of property | Medical equipment, consumables, the recorder and the paper records cabinet are in rooms reached from the corridor. | Equipment list (module 7, A-01 to A-14). The paper records cabinet is locked (SEC-18). |
| Investigating incidents | If something goes missing or someone enters a room they should not, the images show who and when, so the practice can report it to the police. | Not used yet. This is a possible use, and the footage is not browsed without an incident. |

Is the interest legitimate? Yes in principle: protecting people and property is a recognised interest, and it is lawful. The test is whether it is real: it must relate to what the practice actually does and be present now, not hypothetical.

How strong is the evidence? Clinic X has no record of a serious incident. The interest rests on the layout of the premises (an open entrance, an unattended waiting room, records and equipment behind the corridor) and on the vulnerability of the people who wait there. That is a real but modest case. The practice treats it as such: it keeps cameras few, keeps images for a short time, and asks again in December 2027 whether the cameras helped with anything.

### Step 2: are cameras necessary?

| Alternative | Why it is not enough |
|---|---|
| Staff watching. The receptionist at the desk | The receptionist leaves the desk to take patients to the rooms and answers the phone. The waiting room is out of sight from the desk. |
| Door buzzer and controlled entry | It slows entry for unwell patients and for people arriving on foot with children. It does nothing about what happens inside. |
| Locks, locked cabinets, key list | Already in place (SEC-18). They protect records and equipment but not people, and they do not show who did what. |
| Fewer cameras, or recording only outside opening hours | The main risks occur in opening hours. The number was kept to four, and none was added for rooms where care is given. |

Were alternatives tried? The practice did not trial the alternatives before the cameras were installed. It reasoned about them, and the reasoning is above. Art. 5(d) of Law no. 190/2018 speaks of less intrusive means that have not proved effective; whether reasoning without a trial meets that wording is an interpretation point to confirm.

Conclusion on necessity. Cameras in the four common areas are a proportionate way to serve the purposes. Cameras in rooms where care is given are not necessary, and are excluded.

### Step 3: do the people filmed come first?

| Who | What they expect | Effect on them | How it is reduced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patients and visitors | Cameras at the door, yes. In a waiting room of a clinic, some do not expect them, and the images show that someone is at a medical practice. | Their image is recorded in the common areas for 7 days. The setting can suggest that they are receiving care. | Common areas only; no audio; 7 days; one viewer; no live view; clear signs at the entrance and in the waiting room; notice at reception and online. |
| Children and vulnerable patients | Parents expect discretion about their children. | Same as above, with higher sensitivity. | Same. C3 is kept out of line with the toilet door. C3 is the first camera to be reviewed. |
| Employees | Staff are in view of C2, C3 and C4 for the whole working day. They are in a dependent relationship and are not asked for consent. | Constant presence in view. The risk is use of the images to monitor work. | No live view; not used for performance, attendance or conduct monitoring; employee notice; consultation meeting; Art. 5 conditions (section 5). |
| Visitors: providers, couriers, cleaning | Expect cameras at business premises. | Low. | Signs; supervision of providers (SEC-22). |

Conclusion. The practice's interest prevails over the interests and rights of the people filmed, on these conditions: the cameras stay in common areas, with no audio and no analytics; images are kept for 7 days and then overwritten; only Dr. A can view them and only for the stated purposes; nobody is shown live; and the signs and the notice are in place. If any of these changes, the assessment is redone.

Why 7 days? The EDPB guidelines on video devices say footage should in most cases be erased, ideally automatically, after a few days, and that keeping it beyond 72 hours needs more justification. Clinic X is closed at weekends and on public holidays, and a long weekend can pass before an incident is noticed and reported. Seven days covers that, plus the time to look. Longer is not needed. If the monthly check shows that nobody has asked for footage older than 3 days, the period will be shortened.

## 5. Employees: Art. 5 of Law no. 190/2018

Romania adds conditions where employees can be filmed at work. Clinic X applies them although the cameras are not aimed at staff, because staff are in view of three of the four cameras.

|  | Condition (paraphrased) | How Clinic X meets it | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | The employer's legitimate interests are well founded and prevail over the rights and interests of the employees. | Steps 1 to 3 of this LIA. The employer's interest is moderate; the impact on staff is reduced by the rules in section 4. | Met |
| (b) | The employer has informed employees beforehand, completely and explicitly. | Employee notice (module 11), handed out at the meeting of Thu 3 Dec 2026. Cameras were already running; the information was therefore not prior to installation, only to the programme. | Partly |
| (c) | The employer has consulted the union or, where relevant, the employees' representatives before introducing the monitoring. | There is no union and no elected representative: the practice has two employees. Dr. A consulted them directly (section 8). The cameras were installed before the programme, so the consultation came late, and the record says so. | Partly |
| (d) | Other, less intrusive ways of reaching the purpose have not proved effective beforehand. | Step 2 of this LIA. Alternatives were reasoned through, not trialled. | Partly |
| (e) | Storage is proportionate to the purpose and not longer than 30 days, except where the law expressly provides or in well-justified cases. | 7 days; absolute maximum 30 days (RET-06). | Met |

What this means. Two conditions are met, three partly. The gaps are about timing (the system pre-dates the programme) and about proof (alternatives reasoned, not trialled). They cannot be undone, so the practice does what it can now: it documents the position, consults, informs and reviews. Whether Art. 5 reaches a system that is not designed to monitor staff, and how the consultation duty works in a practice with no representatives, are points for a lawyer to confirm.

## 6. Is a DPIA needed?

A DPIA is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk. The Article 29 Working Party guidelines (WP248) list nine criteria; two or more usually point to one. Clinic X meets two and a third partly.

| Criterion (WP248) | Met? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation or scoring | No | Images are not used to rate anyone. |
| Automated decisions with legal or similar effect | No | None. |
| Systematic monitoring | Yes | Cameras record continuously in common areas. |
| Sensitive or highly personal data | Partly | The practice does not aim to record health data, but the setting (a medical practice) can suggest it. |
| Large scale | No | One site, four cameras, a small practice, 7-day buffer. |
| Matching or combining datasets | No | Images are not combined with other data. |
| Vulnerable data subjects | Yes | Patients, children, and employees in a dependent relationship. |
| Innovative technology | No | Standard fixed cameras; no facial recognition or analytics. |
| Prevents people from exercising a right or using a service | No | Cameras do not condition access to care. |

Decision. Two criteria are met and a third partly, so this is a close call. A DPIA is not required on 7 December 2026, because the processing is not on a large scale and the two Romanian list items concern large-scale monitoring. The reasoning is documented here, as Art. 5(2) requires. A DPIA becomes necessary if the practice adds cameras or sites, adds sound, analytics or remote viewing, extends retention beyond 30 days, or puts a camera in an area where care is given. The method for doing one is in module 12.

This is a close call, and a different decision would also be defensible. The point of the threshold check is that the decision is made, reasoned and written down. [Module 12](../12-dpia/) shows what a DPIA looks like when the answer is yes.

## 7. Telling people, and answering them

### Signs and notice

The sign at the entrance and in the waiting room carries the essentials. The full information is in the patient notice (section 7) and at reception. The sign wording follows Annex I of the [patient notice](../02-patient-notice/).

> **VIDEO SURVEILLANCE AREA**
>
> A video surveillance system is used at this location to ensure the safety of patients and staff.
>
> Images are processed in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and stored for a limited period: maximum 7 days.
>
> You have the right of access, the right to object and the right to lodge a complaint with ANSPDCP. More details on data processing are available at reception or on www.clinic-x.example.
>
> Controller: Clinic X (X Medical S.R.L.)
>
> Contact: privacy@clinic-x.example | +40 700 000 000

- Put a sign at the entrance, before a person reaches the first camera, at eye level, and one in the waiting room.
- Make the lettering large enough to read at 2 metres. Use a camera pictogram.
- Make sure the text matches the patient notice (section 7) and the retention schedule (RET-06). Change all three together.
- Do not place signs in the consultation rooms: there are no cameras there. A sign there would suggest otherwise.

**A gap found while writing this module.** The sign says the system is there for the safety of patients and staff. The assessment and the notice also name protection of property. They should say the same thing. The wording is to be aligned at the next reprint, by 31 January 2027, and is recorded as a partial safeguard (G10).

### When someone asks about the images

| Who asks | What the practice does | Who decides | Template |
|---|---|---|---|
| A person asks to see their own image (access, Art. 15) | Ask for date, time slot and area. If the images no longer exist, say so. If they exist, preserve the extract at once, then offer viewing under supervision or a copy with other people masked. | Dr. A | T14 |
| A person objects to the cameras (Art. 21) | Record the grounds. Assess whether the interest overrides. Reply with reasons; if it does not override, stop processing for that person. | Dr. A | T9 |
| The police or another authority asks | Only on a written request that states the legal basis. Check the request. Export the smallest slot needed. Hand it over with a receipt. Log it. | Dr. A, with the external adviser if unsure | No template |
| A victim of an incident asks for a copy (for example, a missing bag) | Do not hand over a copy: it shows other people. Dr. A may look at the images, then advise the person to report to the police. The images go to the police on their written request. | Dr. A | No template |
| Dr. A wants to look at the footage | Only for the stated purposes and only for an incident. Note date, slot, reason and result in the log. No browsing. Never for checking working time, speed or conduct. | Dr. A | Log |

| Step | Who | When |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Recognise it. Anyone asking for images, or complaining about the cameras, is making a request. Reception does not show images or answer on the substance. | Receptionist D | On the spot |
| 2. Write it down. Name, contact, date, time slot, area, what is asked. Pass it to the request handler. | Receptionist D | Within one working day |
| 3. Preserve. If the images are still on the recorder, export the requested slot to the encrypted medium in the locked cabinet so that it is not overwritten before the reply. Log the export. | Dr. A | The same day |
| 4. Check the person. Verify who is asking, as in the rights procedure (module 5). Do not give images to an unverified person. | Dr. A | Before any viewing or copy |
| 5. Decide and reply. Use the template. Mask other people in a copy, or offer viewing under supervision. Record the reply in the request register. | Dr. A | Within one month of receipt |
| 6. Close. Delete the extract when the request is closed, unless a dispute or an authority procedure is open. Note the deletion in the log. | Dr. A | When the request is closed |

**The step people forget is the third: preserve.** Images are overwritten after 7 days. If a request about still-existing images arrives on day 6 and the reply takes three weeks, the images are gone, and the person has been refused by the calendar. Exporting the slot on the day of the request, and deleting it when the request closes, keeps both the right and the retention rule.

### Monthly check

- Once a month, Dr. A opens the recorder, notes the date of the oldest image, and records it in the destruction register. If it is older than 7 days, the overwrite setting is wrong: correct it and record that.
- At the same check: look at the angle of each camera (has it moved?), that the signs are in place, and that the cabinet is locked.
- Count the entries in the log for the month. No entries is normal.

## 11. Design decisions

**1. Limit first, justify second.** Four cameras, common areas, no sound, no live view, seven days. Each limit shrinks the argument that has to be won.

**2. A camera-by-camera decision.** The camera plan gives each camera its own reason, its own impact rating and its own verdict. One decision for the whole system would hide that the waiting room is a harder case than the entrance.

**3. Weak evidence, said out loud.** There has been no incident. The assessment says so, bases the interest on the layout and the people, keeps the period short, and commits to asking again in a year. That is stronger than claiming a risk the practice cannot show.

**4. Seven days, with a reason.** Longer than the EDPB's "few days", shorter than the 30-day legal maximum, and tied to a fact: long weekends. The practice will shorten it if nobody asks for older footage.

**5. Partial is a status, not a failure.** Six of the fourteen safeguards are partly in place, each with an owner and a date. A register where everything is green on day one is less believable.

**6. A close DPIA call, decided and written.** Two criteria met and a third partly. The answer is no, for stated reasons, with triggers that reopen it.

**7. Preserve before you reply.** The footage request procedure copies the requested slot on the day, so that the retention rule cannot defeat the right of access.

**8. Late consultation, recorded as late.** The cameras came first. The consultation record says that, instead of pretending otherwise.

**9. Images are not shown at the desk.** Reception takes the request. Dr. A decides, checks identity and masks other people.

## 13. Blank templates

The blank assessment follows the structure above. Yellow fields are for you to complete; delete the completion notes before use.

**How to use it**

1. Describe your own system: cameras, areas, recorder, retention, who can view. Check the angles in person.
2. For each camera, write the reason and the effect on people. Drop a camera you cannot justify.
3. Write the three steps with real facts. If you have no incident, say what your reason is instead.
4. List the people affected and what reduces the effect on each.
5. Fill the safeguards register with the real status. Add an owner and a date to every partial item.
6. If you have employees, check your national law, and consult them. Record the meeting.
7. Do the DPIA threshold check and write the decision.
8. Put up the signs, adopt the request procedure and the log, and set the date of the first review.
